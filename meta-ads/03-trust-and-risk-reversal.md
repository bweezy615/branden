# 3. Trust & Risk Reversal

← [Pricing](02-pricing-and-money-mechanics.md) · [Index](README.md) · Next: [30-Day Operating Plan](04-30-day-operating-plan.md)

---

**The problem this document solves:** you are asking a small business owner for $1,450
for something that might not work, and you both know it might not work. Pretending
otherwise is the amateur move. Building the uncertainty into the product is the
professional one.

---

## 3.1 The core principle

> **Do not sell certainty you don't have. Sell certainty about the things you actually
> control, and be visibly, unusually honest about the rest.**

You control: the build quality, the daily management, where the money goes, the
reporting, the honesty of your read at the end, and the exit if it's failing.

You do not control: whether people buy.

Every trust mechanism below is a way of moving the sale onto the first list. A client
who understands exactly what you're promising can't be disappointed by you — only by
the market, which is a very different conversation to have in week five.

---

## 3.2 The five mechanisms

Each one is cheap for Marvo and expensive-feeling for the client. That asymmetry is the
whole point.

### 1. The scoreboard, signed before any money is spent

Before launch, both parties sign a one-page
[Scoreboard Agreement](forms/scoreboard-agreement.md) defining what "worked" means —
in numbers, agreed in advance.

**Why it's the most important thing in this document:** without it, "did it work?" gets
decided in week five by whoever is more frustrated. With it, the answer was decided by
both of you while everyone was still optimistic and rational. You are not going to win
a subjective argument about results at the end of a mediocre month. You don't have to
have one.

It also does something subtler: it forces Budget Barn to articulate what they actually
want, and about a third of the time that conversation surfaces that they don't have a
way to *capture* a lead when it arrives — nobody answers the Facebook messages, or
there's no way to tell a walk-in came from an ad. **Better to find that out before you
spend their money than after.**

The scoreboard must define three outcomes, not two:

| Outcome | Meaning | What happens next |
|---|---|---|
| **Worked** | Hit or beat the primary metric | Renewal conversation, day 26 |
| **Inconclusive** | Real signal, not enough volume or a fixable problem | Honest recommendation: one more month or stop. Either is fine. |
| **Didn't work** | Clear miss, no viable path visible | Marvo says so first, plainly, and doesn't pitch month two |

**"Inconclusive" is the row that saves the relationship.** Most 30-day tests on a $750
budget land there. Naming it in advance means a middling result isn't a broken promise
— it's the outcome you both said was likely.

### 2. The day-14 kill switch

At the two-week mark: a sit-down, real numbers, and a genuine option to stop.

> "If it's clearly not working, we stop right there and I send back whatever ad money
> hasn't been spent."

**Cost to Marvo:** near zero. You've collected the full package, the fee is earned by
day 14, and the refund is only unspent media. In practice almost nobody takes it —
by day 14 there's usually *something* interesting on the screen, and the sunk-cost
instinct runs toward finishing.

**Value to the client:** enormous. It's the difference between a $1,450 risk and a
~$725 risk in their head, and it's the single most disarming thing you say in the
meeting. It also proves the thing you claimed at the start: that you'd rather be
honest than paid.

**Do not weaken it with conditions.** No "at my discretion," no fine print. A
conditional guarantee reads as no guarantee and costs you the entire benefit.

### 3. Receipts with every report

Meta's billing receipts, forwarded weekly, showing exactly what hit the platform.

Costs nothing — it's their account. Permanently ends any suspicion about the split.
See [§2.5](02-pricing-and-money-mechanics.md#25-the-honest-answer-script).

### 4. They own the asset

Business Manager, ad account, page, pixel, audiences — all in Budget Barn's name, from
day one. Marvo has partner access, which they can revoke in two clicks.

> "If you fire me at the end of the month, you keep all of it and hand the keys to
> whoever's next. I don't hold your account hostage."

**Why this is worth more than it costs:** holding client accounts hostage is the
single most common complaint small businesses have about marketing people, and most of
them have been burned by it or know someone who has. Volunteering the opposite, before
they ask, marks you as a different category of person in about eight seconds.

It also protects *you*: their account, their card disputes, their compliance history,
their problem. And it makes the DIY objection soft, because you've removed the fear of
getting locked in.

### 5. No contract, no auto-renew

One month. It ends. Renewal is a real conversation on day 26, not a default.

Removes the last structural objection — *"what if I'm stuck with you"* — and it's not
a concession, because a client who renews by choice is worth more than one who renews
by inertia. Inertia clients churn the moment they notice the charge.

## 3.3 Setting expectations without killing the sale

There is a version of honesty that talks you out of the deal. Avoid it. The difference
is **specificity and framing** — honest and confident are not opposites.

| ❌ Kills the sale | ✅ Builds trust |
|---|---|
| "Honestly, this might totally flop." | "I don't know yet if it'll work for you. That's exactly what the thirty days is for." |
| "Facebook ads are really hit or miss." | "I've seen it work great for businesses like yours and I've seen it be a dud. You can't tell which you are without running it properly." |
| "I can't guarantee anything." | "I'll guarantee the work, the money, and the truth. Nobody can guarantee the market." |
| "It might take a few months to see results." | "Thirty days tells us whether there's a signal. If there is, it gets better from there — this is the worst it will ever perform." |
| "The first month is really just testing." | "Month one buys you an answer. Month two buys you customers." |

**The last row is the highest-leverage sentence in this entire document.** It reframes
a weak first month from a failure into the designed outcome — and it makes month two
the obvious next step rather than a second gamble.

### Things to say out loud, before launch, that prevent week-five arguments

State each of these once, plainly, in the meeting or in the kickoff:

- **"The first week or so will look bad, and that's normal."** Meta's system needs
  roughly a week of clean data before it knows what it's doing. Early numbers are
  noise. *Say this before they see the noise, not after they panic about it.*
- **"I'm going to leave it alone in week one on purpose."** Otherwise "you're not
  doing anything" becomes an unspoken resentment.
- **"Some of the ads will lose, and that's the point."** We're running four to find
  the one. Three of them dying is the test working correctly, not the test failing.
- **"You need to actually answer the messages."** If ads generate inquiries and nobody
  responds for two days, the campaign will look broken and it won't be. Agree who
  handles inbound and how fast, in writing, in the scoreboard.
- **"Attribution is imperfect."** Someone might see the ad Tuesday and walk in Saturday
  and never mention Facebook. We'll ask "how'd you hear about us?" and still undercount.
  Say this early — it's the most common source of week-five disputes.

## 3.4 When it fails

It will, some of the time. How you handle a failure determines whether you have a
business or a series of one-month transactions.

### The rules

1. **You say it first.** Never let the client be the one who points out the numbers are
   bad. Call it on the day you see it, not at the scheduled report.
2. **Say it plainly.** No hedging, no burying it under reach and impression stats
   nobody asked about. "This didn't work" is a complete sentence.
3. **Explain what you'd do differently** — briefly, once, without pitching. It shows
   competence and it's genuinely useful to them.
4. **Do not pitch month two.** Not in the same conversation. If they ask, answer
   honestly. If they don't, let it go.
5. **Hand over everything cleanly.** Account access confirmed, assets in their
   possession, a short written summary of what was tested and what the data said. Make
   the exit as professional as the entry.
6. **Ask for the honest post-mortem.** "Is there anything I should've done differently
   on my end?" Sometimes you learn something. Always you leave a good impression.

### The script

> "I want to give you the straight version. The numbers aren't there. We got
> [X reach / Y clicks / Z inquiries] against the [N] we said we were aiming for,
> and I don't see a path to [N] at this budget with what I'm looking at.
>
> Here's my honest read on why: [one or two specific things — the audience was too
> broad, the offer wasn't strong enough for a cold audience, the budget was too thin
> to get out of the learning phase, whatever it actually was].
>
> If you ever want to try again — different season, different offer, bigger budget —
> I'd be glad to. But I'm not going to sit here and sell you another month on the back
> of a bad one. Everything's in your account and it's yours. Anything I can do to make
> the handoff clean?"

### Why this pays

A client you were honest with after a failure will:

- **Refer you.** "He told me straight up it didn't work and gave me my money back on
  the part he hadn't spent" is a better referral than most successes generate. In a
  local business community this travels.
- **Come back.** Businesses change — new season, new product, new competitor. A
  significant share of failed tests call again within eighteen months.
- **Not damage you.** A local owner who feels burned tells everyone, and in a small
  market that's fatal. The refund and the honest call cost you a few hundred dollars
  and buy you the ability to keep selling in that town.

**The whole model depends on this.** You are selling a product that fails a meaningful
percentage of the time. The only way that's a sustainable business rather than a churn
machine is if failing clients leave with a good opinion of you. Budget for that
deliberately: the day-14 refund and the honest post-mortem are not generosity, they are
customer acquisition cost for everyone those people talk to.
