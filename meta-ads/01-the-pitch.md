# 1. The Pitch

← [Back to index](README.md) · Next: [Pricing & Money Mechanics](02-pricing-and-money-mechanics.md)

---

## 1.1 The reframe — read this before anything else

Most people selling Meta ads to a small business walk in selling **advertising**.
That's a losing position, because the buyer immediately starts comparing you to their
nephew, to Canva, to the "boost post" button, and to doing nothing. All three of those
are cheaper than you.

Don't sell advertising. Sell **the answer to a question they already have and can't
resolve.**

Budget Barn's owner has already wondered whether Facebook and Instagram ads would
bring people in. They've probably wondered it more than once. Right now that question
is unanswered, and an unanswered question about your own growth is expensive — it sits
there for years while competitors either prove it or don't.

> **The sale is:** "You've wondered whether this works for you. For $1,450 and thirty
> days, you'll know for certain instead of guessing for another two years."

This single move does four things at once:

1. **It makes the price small.** $1,450 to settle a question worth tens of thousands
   of dollars of future decisions is obviously cheap. $1,450 for "some Facebook ads"
   is obviously expensive. Same money, different frame.
2. **It survives failure.** If the ads don't produce sales, the product still got
   delivered — they got their answer. You are not on the hook for an outcome you
   don't control.
3. **It kills the DIY objection.** They can't answer the question themselves, because
   they don't know what a good cost-per-result looks like and they'd never run it long
   enough or clean enough to get a real signal.
4. **It sets up month two.** If the answer is yes, the next conversation is obvious
   and easy. You already built the machine.

**Never promise sales. Never promise a return. Promise a clean test and a real
answer.** Everything below is built on that.

## 1.2 Before the meeting — 20 minutes of homework

Walk in having done this. It is the single biggest difference between a $1,450 close
and a "let me think about it."

- [ ] **Pull up their Facebook and Instagram.** Last post date. Follower count.
      Engagement. Whether the page info is even correct.
- [ ] **Check the Meta Ad Library** (`facebook.com/ads/library`) for Budget Barn and
      for 2–3 local competitors. This is public and free. Find out who in their
      market is *currently running ads.*
- [ ] **Screenshot one competitor's live ad.** This is your best prop. Nothing sells
      like "here's what your competitor is spending money on right now, today."
- [ ] **Know their average ticket and what they actually sell.** Ask on the phone
      when you book the meeting if you don't know.
- [ ] **Have the one-page proposal printed.** Paper. Not a deck, not a PDF link.

**The prop matters more than the script.** A phone turned around showing a competitor's
live ad does more work than ten minutes of talking.

## 1.3 The script

Ten to twelve minutes. Do not rush the silence after the price.

### Open — 30 seconds, no pitch

> "Thanks for the time. Before I show you anything — how are people finding Budget
> Barn right now? Like, if I'm a customer who's never heard of you, what's the path
> that gets me in the door?"

**Then stop talking.** Whatever they say is the raw material for the rest of the
meeting. Take an actual note on actual paper. Listen for: word of mouth, drive-by,
"we've been here twenty years," a slow season, a competitor they're annoyed about.

Follow-ups, pick one or two:

> "How's that been trending the last year or two?"
> "Have you ever tried anything paid — Facebook, Google, the paper?"
> "Is there a season where you'd love to be busier than you are?"

### The prop — 60 seconds

Turn the phone around.

> "So here's something you can look up yourself, it's public. This is Meta's ad
> library — every ad running on Facebook and Instagram, anyone can see them. This is
> [competitor], running right now, today. And this one. Neither of these is a
> better business than yours. They're just visible in a place where you're not.
>
> And here's the thing — I looked, and Budget Barn isn't in here at all. Which means
> every person in this area scrolling their phone tonight is seeing them and not you.
> That's not a marketing problem. That's just a gap."

### The honest frame — 90 seconds

This is where you win or lose the trust. Say the risky part first, out loud, before
they can think it.

> "Now — I want to be straight with you about something, because I'd rather lose the
> sale than have you feel misled in six weeks.
>
> **I don't know if this will work for Budget Barn.** Nobody does. Anyone who sits
> across from you and promises you a return on Facebook ads is either lying or hasn't
> done it very long. I've seen it work incredibly well for businesses like yours and
> I've seen it be a dud, and the honest truth is you cannot tell which one you are
> until you actually run it properly for about thirty days.
>
> So that's exactly what I'm proposing. Not a contract. Not a retainer. **One month,
> one price, run properly, and at the end you'll know.** And you'll own everything we
> build either way."

*Note the move: you have just said the scariest sentence in the room, first, on your
own terms. Everything after this lands differently. This is not a weakness play — it
is the strongest thing you will say all meeting.*

### What they actually get — 90 seconds

Keep it concrete. Don't say "optimization" or "funnel" or "creative strategy."

> "Here's what the thirty days looks like.
>
> **Week one**, I build it. Your ad account set up properly under your name so you own
> it — I'll explain that in a second. Three or four different ads, different pictures,
> different wording, because I don't know which one your customers respond to and
> neither do you. We aim them at people within [X] miles of this building who look
> like the people who already shop here.
>
> **Then I leave it alone for a week**, which sounds lazy but it's the most important
> part — Meta's system needs about a week of clean data before it knows what it's
> doing. Most people who try this themselves panic on day three and change everything,
> and that's exactly why it didn't work for them.
>
> **Day fourteen we sit down**, ten minutes, and I show you the real numbers. Not a
> pretty report — the actual dashboard. And if it's clearly not working, we stop
> right there and I refund whatever ad money hasn't been spent yet. I'd rather do that
> than take another two weeks of your money on something dying.
>
> **Weeks three and four**, I kill the ads that lost and put all the money behind
> whichever one won. That's where most of the results come from.
>
> **End of month, you get a plain-English report** — what it cost, what it got, and my
> honest read on whether you should keep going. And if my answer is 'don't,' I'll say
> don't."

### The ownership move — 30 seconds

This is a trust builder most sellers skip. It costs you nothing and it's worth a lot.

> "One more thing. Everything gets built in **your** business account, under your
> name. Your ad account, your page, your tracking, your audience data. I get added as
> a manager the same way you'd give a bookkeeper access.
>
> Meaning if you fire me at the end of the month, or in two years, **you keep all of
> it** and hand the keys to whoever's next. I don't hold your account hostage.
> A lot of people in my line of work do exactly that, and it's the main reason
> business owners hate marketing people."

### The price — 45 seconds, then shut up

> "So — thirty days, everything included, **is fourteen fifty.**
>
> And I want to be clear on what 'everything included' means, because this is where
> most people get surprised later. **That's the ad money too.** You're not paying me
> a fee and then getting a separate bill from Facebook. There's no second invoice,
> there's no 'we need more budget' phone call in week three. One number, one time,
> and it's handled. Whatever it costs to run, it costs me, not you.
>
> After thirty days, if it worked, we talk about month two. If it didn't, you owe me
> nothing further and you keep the account."

**Now stop. Completely. Do not fill the silence.**

The first person to speak after a price loses. Count to ten in your head if you have
to. If they're quiet, they're doing math — that's good, let them finish.

### The close

Don't ask "what do you think?" That invites a stall. Ask a scheduling question:

> "If it makes sense, I'd want to start early next week so we've got the full thirty
> days before [relevant date — holiday, season, whatever they mentioned]. Does that
> timing work on your end, or is there a better window?"

Or the assumptive version:

> "Want me to write it up? I've got the one-pager here — it's a page, not a contract
> you need a lawyer for. Take a day with it if you want."

## 1.4 Objection handling

Nine objections. The first three cover ~70% of real ones.

### Objection 1 — "That's more than I expected." / "That's a lot of money."

Don't discount. Don't defend. Break the number down and shrink the unit.

> "It is real money, and I'd rather you feel that than not. Let me put it next to
> something.
>
> That's about **forty-eight dollars a day** for a month. What does a day of the
> newspaper or a radio spot run you around here — and can you tell me afterward
> exactly how many people saw it and what they did? Because I can, down to the person.
>
> And here's the other half of it: this is the **only** month it costs this much to
> find out. Whatever we learn in these thirty days — which picture worked, which words
> worked, which customers responded — you own that forever, whether you work with me
> again or not."

**If they still stall**, do not cut the price. Cut the scope — offer the $895 Test
Drive tier instead. Cutting price teaches them your prices are fake. Cutting scope
teaches them your prices are real.

> "If fourteen fifty is more than you want to risk on an experiment, I've got a
> smaller version — eight ninety-five, less ad money behind it, so it takes longer to
> get a clean read and it's a rougher signal. It's a real test, just a quieter one.
> Honestly? I'd rather sell you the smaller one and have it be your idea than talk you
> into the bigger one."

### Objection 2 — "How much of that actually goes to Facebook?"

**Answer it. Honestly. Immediately. Do not dodge, do not get vague, do not change the
subject.** This is the single highest-leverage moment in the entire meeting, and
sellers blow it constantly by getting squirrelly.

> "About half. Roughly seven-fifty of it goes on the platform as actual ad money, and
> the rest is me — building it, writing it, running it every day for a month, and
> reporting on it.
>
> And you don't have to take my word for that. **I'll send you the Meta billing
> receipts with every weekly report**, straight from the platform, so you can see to
> the dollar what landed on the ads. If I'm ever off from what I told you, you'll know
> before I do."

Then, if they're still chewing on it, land the frame:

> "I'll say the obvious thing — yes, you could put that seven-fifty on Facebook
> yourself tomorrow and save my half. Some people should. But the money isn't the
> hard part, it's the smallest part. The hard part is knowing what to aim it at, and
> knowing what the numbers mean when they come back. Spending seven-fifty badly is
> just a slower way to lose seven-fifty."

*Why this works: the fear behind the question isn't "you're making money." Everyone
knows you're making money. The fear is "you're making money and hiding it from me."
Volunteering the receipts kills that permanently, and it costs you nothing because the
receipts are already in the account they own.*

### Objection 3 — "What if it doesn't work?"

Never flinch. You already pre-empted this, so this is a callback, not a save.

> "Then it doesn't work, and you'll know that by day fourteen instead of guessing
> about it for the next five years. That's genuinely worth something.
>
> Three things protect you here. **One** — we agree in writing, before I spend a
> dollar, on what 'working' means. Not vibes. An actual number we both sign off on, so
> at the end there's no argument about whether it went well. **Two** — day fourteen,
> if it's clearly dead, we stop and I send back the ad money we haven't spent. **Three**
> — there's no month two. No contract, no auto-renew. If it flops, you never hear from
> me again unless you call.
>
> The only thing I can actually guarantee is that it'll be run properly and you'll get
> the truth about the result. If I could guarantee sales I'd charge you a lot more
> than fourteen fifty."

### Objection 4 — "I tried boosting posts before and it did nothing." *(the burned-before track)*

Good news: this objection is a buying signal in disguise. They already believe in the
channel enough to have tried.

> "Yeah — and I'd have been surprised if it had worked, honestly.
>
> Boosting a post is Meta's 'give us money' button. It takes whatever you already
> posted, shows it to a vague audience, and optimizes for likes — because likes look
> good in the app. Likes are not customers. I've never seen a boosted post move real
> revenue for a business like yours.
>
> What I'm describing is a different system entirely. Purpose-built ads, aimed at
> people near you who match your actual customers, optimized for someone *contacting
> you or walking in* — not for a thumbs up. Same platform, completely different
> machine.
>
> The fact that you already tried actually helps me. What did you spend, and what
> happened? That tells me something."

### Objection 5 — "My nephew / my daughter / a kid I know can do this."

Do not insult the nephew. Ever. You'll lose the room instantly.

> "They might do a fine job — and if they'll do it for free, seriously, that's hard
> to beat on price.
>
> Two things I'd think about. **One**, this is a daily thing for about the first two
> weeks, not a weekend project, and family favors tend to go quiet around day nine.
> **Two**, the platform isn't really the skill anymore — the buttons are easy. The
> skill is looking at a screen that says forty-one dollars per result and knowing
> whether that's great or terrible for a business like yours. That comes from having
> watched a lot of accounts, not from being good at Instagram.
>
> Here's what I'd actually suggest. Let me run the thirty days. If it works, **have
> them sit in on the handoff** and I'll show them exactly how it's built — it's your
> account, they'd inherit a working machine instead of a blank page. That's a better
> use of them than making them figure it out from zero on your dime."

### Objection 6 — "Can I just pay you the fee and run the ad spend myself?"

Yes, and it's usually fine — but price it so the bundle stays attractive.

> "We can do it that way. You'd put your card on the ad account, I'd bill you nine
> fifty for the month's work, and you'd be paying Meta directly.
>
> Fair warning on why I usually don't: you'd get charged by Meta in a bunch of small
> odd amounts spread over the month — it's not one clean bill — and new ad accounts
> sometimes get payment holds that stop the ads dead until someone fixes them. That's
> a phone call I'd rather handle than have you handle. It's also why I'd rather just
> eat that whole mess for you and give you one number.
>
> But if you'd rather have your own card on it and see every charge, that's completely
> reasonable and I'll set it up that way."

*Note the pricing: $950 fee + their ~$750 spend = ~$1,700 of their money, more than
the $1,450 bundle. Not a punishment — an honest reflection of you no longer carrying
the float, the billing hassle, or the payment-hold risk. Most people take the bundle.*

### Objection 7 — "Why only one month? Everyone else wants six."

> "Because I don't think you should sign a six-month deal with someone you've never
> worked with, on a channel that hasn't proven itself for your business yet. That's
> two unknowns stacked on top of each other.
>
> If it works, month two is an easy conversation and I'd love to have it. If it
> doesn't, a six-month contract would just mean you're stuck paying for five more
> months of something we both already know isn't working. I'd rather earn it monthly."

### Objection 8 — "Let me think about it." / "Let me talk to my partner."

Never fight this. Convert it into a date.

> "Of course — I'd think about it too, and honestly if you said yes in the room I'd
> wonder if you were really listening.
>
> Leave the page with you. One thing I'd ask: **give me a day.** Not to pressure you,
> just so it doesn't sit on the counter for three weeks. Is Thursday morning or
> Thursday afternoon better for a five-minute call either way? Even if the answer's
> no, I'd rather hear it than wonder."

Then actually call Thursday. Most of this business is calling Thursday.

### Objection 9 — "Can you guarantee results?"

> "No. And I'd walk out of here if someone told you they could.
>
> Nobody controls whether people buy — not me, not Meta. What I'll guarantee in
> writing is the work: the ads get built, they run the full thirty days, the money
> goes where I said it goes, you see the receipts, and you get the truth at the end
> even when the truth is 'this didn't work.'
>
> That's the honest version of a guarantee, and it's the only one worth anything."

## 1.5 Rules for Marco

Non-negotiable, in order of how much money they'll save you.

1. **Never promise a result.** Not "you'll get customers," not "usually we see," not
   a wink. Promise process, effort, honesty, and reporting. Nothing else.
2. **Say the risk out loud before they do.** The person who names the downside first
   owns the credibility in the room.
3. **Say the price, then stop talking.** Every additional word after a price is a
   discount you're offering for free.
4. **Never discount. Cut scope instead.** A discounted price tells them the first
   price was a lie, and it prices every future month lower forever.
5. **Answer the "how much goes to Meta" question straight, every time.** It's a trust
   test, not a math question.
6. **100% of the money before a single ad runs.** No exceptions, no "we'll settle up
   after," not for a nice guy, not for a friend of a friend.
7. **Never take their password.** Ever. Partner access through Business Manager only.
   Taking a password makes you liable for everything that ever happens to that account.
8. **Book the renewal conversation on day 26**, while ads are still live and the
   momentum is real — not day 30 when everything has already gone quiet.
9. **If it fails, say so first and say so plainly.** The client who trusts you after a
   failure is worth more than the one who never tested you. Some of them will come
   back in a year, and some of them will send you someone else.
