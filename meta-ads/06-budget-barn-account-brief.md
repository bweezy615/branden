## 6.1 What Budget Barn actually is

**This is not a walk-in retail store, and that changes the entire pitch.**

Budget Barn TN is a **liquidation auction business.** They buy customer returns,
overstock, and business buyouts from major retailers, break them into individual
lots, and sell them through **online auctions hosted on HiBid**
(`budgetbarn.hibid.com`, HiBid company ID 149798).

## 6.3 Why this business is an unusually good fit for Meta ads

Say these out loud in the meeting. They are specific to Budget Barn and they are
things a generic ad seller would never know.

### 1. They have real, recurring urgency — for free

Almost every local business advertising on Meta has to *manufacture* a reason to act
now. Budget Barn has one built into the business model: **every auction has a closing
time.** "Lots start closing Monday at 5pm" is genuine, honest urgency, it renews every
single week, and it never gets stale.

This is the single strongest structural advantage in the account.

### 2. More bidders raise the price on inventory they already own

This is the argument that should close the deal, and it's specific to auctions.

For a normal retailer, more traffic means more sales. For an auction house, more
bidders means **more sales *and* higher clearing prices on the same lots.** Budget
Barn has already paid for that pallet. Every additional serious bidder on a lot raises
the final hammer price — and their 15% buyer's premium scales with it.

> "You've already bought the inventory. The pallet costs what it costs whether four
> people bid on it or forty. Ads don't just bring you more buyers — they raise what
> the same box sells for."

### 3. A bidder is a repeat asset, not a one-time sale

A registered bidder doesn't buy once. They come back auction after auction, for years.
So the honest math isn't "cost per sale" — it's **cost to acquire a bidder who bids
every week for the next two years.** That makes the ad math far more forgiving than
it looks, and it's the honest reason a month-one test can look mediocre and still be
a win.

### 4. The content is free and it's the best-performing format on the platform

Liquidation, pallet-unboxing, and bin-store content is one of the highest-engagement
categories on Meta and TikTok, full stop. Budget Barn is sitting on infinite native
content and it costs nothing to produce:

- "This retailed for $340. Bidding starts at $1."
- Unboxing a fresh pallet on camera
- Warehouse walk-throughs before an auction goes live
- Winner reactions and haul shots
- Category teasers — tools day, baby day, electronics day

**Most local businesses have a creative problem. Budget Barn has a creative surplus.**
That directly lowers the cost of every ad in the account.

### 5. Nationwide shipping removes the geography ceiling

Most local advertisers are capped by a driving radius. Budget Barn ships worldwide, so
if local converts, the same ads can scale to a much larger audience without changing
the business at all. That is a real month-three conversation and worth naming early.

## 6.4 The problems — name these honestly, before Andrew does

### ⚠️ The tracking problem (most important — read this one twice)

**Budget Barn does not own HiBid.** The auction, the registration, and the bidding all
happen on a third-party platform. That almost certainly means **the Meta pixel cannot
be installed on the pages where conversions actually occur.**

Practically: you may be unable to track "registered as a bidder" or "placed a bid"
as a Meta conversion event, which means Meta's system can't optimize toward it and
Budget Barn can't see it in a report.

**This must be resolved before quoting a scoreboard metric.** Options, best first:

1. **Ask HiBid directly whether sellers can add a tracking pixel.** Some auction
   platforms support this for their sellers. Free if the answer is yes, and it's a
   five-minute email. **Do this before the meeting if there's time.**
2. **Pixel on budgetbarntn.com** (which they do own) and treat *"clicked through to the
   live auction"* as the tracked conversion. Imperfect but real, and it's optimizable.
3. **Build an owned list instead** — run Meta lead ads or click-to-Messenger offering
   *"get a text the moment the next auction goes live."* Fully trackable inside Meta,
   optimizable, and it produces an **asset Budget Barn keeps forever** and can market
   to for free. **This is probably the smartest month-one play** regardless of what
   HiBid says.
4. **Manual attribution** — HiBid's own new-bidder counts per auction, compared week
   over week against ad spend. Crude, but it's a real signal and Andrew already has
   the data.

**Recommended primary metric for the scoreboard: new registered bidders + list
sign-ups, with clicks to the auction as the tracked proxy.** Not sales. Sales are
downstream of a platform Marvo doesn't control, and promising a number you can't
measure is how the month-five argument starts.

### ⚠️ The friction points

- **15% buyer's premium + tax.** Not unusual for auctions, but if the ad says "starting
  at $1" and the invoice comes in higher than expected, that's a complaint. **Ads should
  mention the premium** — honest ads convert worse in week one and much better over a
  year, and it protects the page from angry comments that suppress reach.
- **All sales final, as-is.** Correct for the model, but it raises the trust bar for a
  cold audience who's never bought from them. This is exactly why **social proof
  creative** (real winners, real hauls, real reviews) should be one of the four test
  variations rather than an afterthought.
- **HiBid registration is a real funnel step.** Cold traffic has to leave Meta, land on
  HiBid, create an account, and put a card on file before bidding. That's meaningful
  drop-off and it will make raw click-to-bidder rates look bad. **Set that expectation
  in the scoreboard, in writing, before launch.**
- **Thin warm audience (~388 followers).** Retargeting will be weak in month one and
  much stronger by month two. Another honest reason renewals matter here.

## 6.5 Recommended campaign structure

Tuned to the auction model — this replaces the generic local-retail structure in
[Section 4](04-30-day-operating-plan.md).

### Objective

**Traffic to the live auction + lead capture for the auction-alert list.** Not reach,
not engagement, not "boost the post."

### Audiences

| # | Audience | Why |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Gallatin + Sumner County + Nashville metro, ~45 min radius** | The pickup buyers. Highest intent, no shipping friction, cheapest to convert. |
| 2 | **Broad national, interest-layered** — liquidation, pallet deals, bargain hunting, reselling, flipping | Shipping buyers. Bigger pool, and this is the resale community that bids hardest. |
| 3 | **Warm** — page/IG engagers + website visitors + video viewers | Small now, but the cheapest results in the account and it compounds all month. |

**Note on audience 2:** resellers and flippers are a real, identifiable, high-value
segment for a liquidation auction. They bid repeatedly, they bid on volume, and they
don't need convincing about the model. Worth testing deliberately rather than hoping
they show up.

### Creative — 4 variations

| # | Format | Angle | Testing |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Video — pallet unboxing** | "Look what just came in" | Does the surprise mechanic carry it? *(expected winner)* |
| 2 | **Video — Andrew talking to camera** | "Auction closes Monday at 5" | Does a face + a deadline carry it? |
| 3 | **Static/carousel — retail-price contrast** | "$340 retail. Bidding starts at $1." | Is raw price the lever? |
| 4 | **Social proof** | Real winner, real haul, real review | Does credibility beat novelty for a cold audience with an all-sales-final policy? |

### Timing — the thing that makes this account different

**Budget Barn's ad calendar should be pinned to the auction calendar, not spread flat
across the month.** Push spend into the 48–72 hours before lots start closing, when
urgency is real and bids are live. Flat daily spend on an auction business wastes money
on days when there's nothing to bid on.

This is the highest-leverage single decision in the account, and it's the kind of thing
only somebody who actually looked at the business would say in the meeting.

## 6.6 Talking points for the meeting

Five specific things a generic seller could never say. Use two or three, not all five.

1. > "You've got something almost none of my other clients have — a real deadline
   > every single week. I don't have to invent a reason for people to act now. Your
   > auction closing Monday at five *is* the reason."

2. > "Here's what I think most people miss about advertising an auction. You've already
   > paid for that pallet. It costs you the same whether four people bid or forty. More
   > bidders don't just mean more sales — they raise what the same box sells for, and
   > your fifteen percent goes up with it."

3. > "A bidder isn't a sale, it's a subscriber. Somebody who registers this month is
   > still bidding next January. So I'm not really trying to buy you sales in thirty
   > days — I'm trying to buy you bidders, and then they pay you back every week for
   > years."

4. > "One thing I want to flag before you pay me anything, because it'll matter. Your
   > bidding happens on HiBid, and you don't own HiBid — so I may not be able to track
   > all the way to a registration. I need to check what's possible there before we
   > set a target. I'd rather tell you that now than explain it in week four."

5. > "The content is the easy part for you. Unboxing a pallet on camera is the single
   > best-performing kind of video on the platform right now, and you generate it every
   > week just doing your job. Most businesses I work with have to invent something
   > worth filming."
