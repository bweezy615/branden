# 6. Financial Model

> **These are planning numbers built on national averages.** Three inputs must be replaced
> with real local quotes in week one — your **workers' comp rate**, your **insurance
> premium**, and your **local labor wage** — because all three move the model materially.

> ### Do not cut working capital to buy equipment.
> This is the mistake that kills first-year contractors, and it kills them while their
> order book is full. You pay crews every week and buy materials up front. Homeowners pay
> on draws; insurance carriers pay in 30–60 days. That gap is real, it is large, and it
> does not care how profitable your jobs are. The base case below shows the company
> running a **cumulative operating deficit that bottoms out around –$38,000 in month 5**,
> *before* accounting for receivables timing. $95,000 of working capital is the honest
> number. A nicer truck is not.
>
> **Also negotiate a $50,000 line of credit** in month one, while the balance sheet still
> shows the full capital injection. Banks lend to companies that don't need money. Draw
> on it only for receivables timing, never for operating losses.

## 6.2 Revenue build — Year 1 base case

### The emergency funnel, stated honestly

The "25% conversion" figure means **25% of *qualified* emergency calls** — calls involving
damage that actually requires reconstruction. A jammed door or a loose deck rail is a
legitimate emergency ticket but there is no rebuild behind it. Roughly 35% of emergency
calls are qualified.

```
  365 emergency calls received in Year 1
    ↓  ~35% involve damage requiring reconstruction
  128 qualified rebuild opportunities
    ↓  60% → estimate delivered within 3 days
   77 estimates presented
    ↓  42% → signed contract
   32 rebuild contracts won
    ↓  30–90 day lag from call to billing
  ~22 contracts billed within Year 1  ·  avg $14,000  =  $308,000
```

The other 10 signed contracts bill in Year 2 — which is exactly why Year 2 opens strong.

### Revenue by source

| Source | Volume | Avg ticket | Year 1 revenue |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Emergency mitigation & repair | 365 calls | $850 | $310,250 |
| Rebuilds converted from emergency calls | 22 contracts | $14,000 | $308,000 |
| Direct-channel projects (search, referral, reviews) | 12 contracts | $13,500 | $162,000 |
| **Total** | | | **$780,250** |

**Seasonality is real and this ramp partly disguises it.** Emergency volume spikes with
weather — freeze events, storm season, heavy rain. Deck and exterior work concentrates in
warm months. Expect your actual curve to be lumpier than this smooth ramp, with weather
weeks running 3–4x normal emergency volume. **Plan crew capacity and cash for the spikes,
and use the slow weeks for backlog remodeling.** That counter-cyclicality between the two
divisions is a real structural advantage — use it deliberately.

## 6.4 Break-even

**Fixed overhead:** $302,600/yr = **$25,217/month**
**Blended gross margin:** 42%

```
  Monthly break-even revenue  =  $25,217 ÷ 0.42  =  $60,040
  Annual break-even revenue   =  $720,476
```

**Two dates to put in front of the investor:**
- **Month 6** — monthly break-even. The company stops losing money.
- **Month 11** — cumulative break-even. The company has earned back everything it lost.

**And one warning:** the cumulative operating deficit bottoms at **–$37,969**, but *cash*
runs meaningfully worse than that, because receivables lag revenue by 30–60 days and the
startup capex hits in month one. This table is the single clearest justification for the
$95,000 working capital line.

## 6.5 Cash flow discipline

Profit and cash are different, and it's the second one that kills you. The controls:

| Control | Effect on cash |
|---|---|
| Deposits collected before mobilization | Customer funds materials, not you |
| Invoice the day a milestone is hit | Removes 5–15 days of lag, every job |
| Materials on the business credit card | 30–45 days of free float |
| Sub payments net 15–30 | Aligns outflow behind inflow |
| Weekly AR review, hard 60-day rule | Prevents receivables aging into losses |
| $50K line of credit, drawn only for timing | Covers the gap without diluting equity |
| Taxes set aside weekly in a separate account | Prevents the April surprise |
| 10% retainage held on subs over $5,000 | Cash buffer + quality leverage |

**Watch backlog, not just revenue.** Backlog = contracted work not yet started. It is your
forward visibility. If backlog is falling while revenue is rising, you are consuming your
pipeline and revenue will fall off a cliff in 60 days. Most contractors notice this far
too late.

## 6.6 Unit economics — why this model works

### Cost to acquire an emergency call

```
  Marketing spend, Year 1        $46,800
  Emergency calls generated          365
  ─────────────────────────────────────────
  COST PER EMERGENCY CALL           $128
```

### Value of one emergency call (steady state, no ramp lag)

| | Amount |
|---|---:|
| Mitigation revenue | $850 |
| Expected rebuild revenue<br>*(32 contracts ÷ 365 calls = 8.8% × $14,000)* | $1,232 |
| **Total expected revenue per call** | **$2,082** |
| × 42% gross margin | |
| **Expected gross profit per call** | **$874** |
| Less: CAC | ($128) |
| Less: technician conversion bonus (8.8% × $250) | ($22) |
| **Net contribution per emergency call** | **$724** |

```
  RETURN ON ACQUISITION SPEND  =  $874 ÷ $128  =  6.8x
```

### The thesis, in one comparison

| | Emergency only | Emergency + rebuild |
|---|---:|---:|
| Revenue per call | $850 | $2,082 |
| Gross profit per call | $357 | $874 |
| Return on CAC | 2.8x | **6.8x** |

**A 2.8x return on acquisition spend does not survive 39% overhead.** A 6.8x return does.
The rebuild conversion isn't a bonus on top of the emergency business — **it is the reason
the emergency business is viable at all.** If conversion falls below ~15%, the model breaks
and you are running an expensive handyman service. This is why conversion is on the weekly
scorecard and why the technician bonus gets paid same-week without argument.

## 6.7 Sensitivity analysis

| Scenario | Revenue | GM% | Overhead | **Net income** | **Net %** |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| **Downside** — slow ramp, 15% conversion, margin slippage | $600,000 | 38% | $290,000 | **($62,000)** | **(10.3%)** |
| **Base** | $780,250 | 42% | $302,600 | **$25,105** | **3.2%** |
| **Upside** — strong weather year, 32% conversion, disciplined costing | $950,000 | 45% | $320,000 | **$107,500** | **11.3%** |

**The downside case is survivable and that's the point of the capital structure.** A
$62,000 loss against $250,000 of capital leaves the company alive with roughly $188,000
of runway to correct. It does not leave the company alive if working capital was spent on
trucks.

### What each variable is worth

| Lever | Move | Annual profit impact |
|---|---|---:|
| **Gross margin** | +1 point | **+$7,800** |
| **Crew utilization** | 70% → 75% | **+$14,000** |
| **Conversion rate** | 25% → 30% | **+$27,000** |
| **Call answer rate** | 90% → 98% | **+$61,000** |
| Emergency ticket | +$100 | +$36,500 |
| Overhead | –5% | +$15,100 |

> **Look at that top line again. Answering the phone is worth more than everything else
> on this list.** Going from 90% to 98% live answer rate is worth roughly $61,000 a year
> in revenue at a cost of about $250 a month. There is no other lever in this business
> with that ratio, and it requires no skill, no capital, and no market conditions — only
> the discipline to never let a call go unanswered.

## 6.8 Years 2 and 3

| | Year 1 | Year 2 | Year 3 |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Revenue | $780,250 | $1,350,000 | $2,000,000 |
| Gross margin | 42.0% | 43.0% | 44.0% |
| Gross profit | $327,705 | $580,500 | $880,000 |
| Overhead | $302,600 | $410,000 | $560,000 |
| **Net operating income** | **$25,105** | **$170,500** | **$320,000** |
| **Net margin** | **3.2%** | **12.6%** | **16.0%** |
| Crews | 2 | 3–4 | 5–6 |
| Headcount | 7 | 12 | 18 |

**Why margin expands:**
- Overhead is largely fixed and stops scaling with revenue — this is the whole story
- Year 2 opens with ~10 signed contracts carried over from Year 1 plus real backlog
- Review count and search ranking compound; CAC falls as organic and referral share grows
- Adjuster relationships mature into inbound work at near-zero acquisition cost
- Job costing data from Year 1 makes Year 2 estimates materially more accurate
- Crews get faster on repeated work types; utilization climbs toward 75%

**Year 3 optionality:** a second service territory, HomeGuard recurring revenue at scale,
or light commercial (property managers, small multi-family). Do not chase any of these
until the core model is clean and repeatable in one metro. Contractors who expand at 3%
net margin expand their losses.

## 6.9 Investor returns

Base case, $250,000 invested with the 8% preferred return and 35% Class B equity from
[§2.3](02-formation-and-ownership.md#23-ownership-structure):

| Year | Net operating income | Available to distribute | Capital returned | Cumulative to investor |
|---:|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| 1 | $25,105 | ~$18,000 | $18,000 | $18,000 |
| 2 | $170,500 | ~$130,000 | $130,000 | $148,000 |
| 3 | $320,000 | ~$240,000 | $102,000 + pref | ~$270,000 |

**Full capital return plus the preferred lands in Year 3.** From that point the investor's
35% runs against a business producing $320K+ annually, and they hold equity in a company
with real enterprise value — residential contractors with clean books, recurring revenue,
and documented systems trade in the 3–5x EBITDA range.

**A $250,000 investment against a company worth $1.0–1.6M in Year 3, with 35% ownership,
is roughly $350K–$560K of equity value on top of capital already returned.**

That case rests entirely on the systems in [Section 4](04-operations-manual.md) being run
as written. Contractors without job costing, documented processes, and clean books don't
sell for a multiple — they sell for the value of the trucks.
