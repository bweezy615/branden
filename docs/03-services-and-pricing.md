## 3.1 Division A — Emergency Home Repair (24/7)

### What we take

| Service | Typical trigger | Ticket range |
|---|---|---|
| Water intrusion response | Burst/frozen pipe, supply line failure, appliance leak | $600 – $3,500 |
| Emergency board-up | Break-in, vandalism, broken window/door, vehicle impact | $250 – $600 per opening |
| Roof tarp / emergency dry-in | Wind, hail, fallen limb, missing shingles | $400 – $1,200 |
| Storm & tree impact response | Tree through roof/wall, downed limb damage | $800 – $6,000 |
| Structural shoring & stabilization | Sagging floor, failed beam, deck/stair collapse | $900 – $5,000 |
| Emergency door/lock/entry repair | Failed door, forced entry, lockout damage | $250 – $900 |
| Deck & stair emergency repair | Collapsed rail, rotted stringer, failed ledger | $400 – $2,400 |
| Emergency securing (general) | Anything making a house unsafe or unsecured tonight | $300 – $1,500 |
| Ceiling/drywall collapse response | Water-loaded ceiling, partial collapse | $500 – $2,500 |

**Blended average emergency ticket: ~$850.** That number drives the whole revenue model.

### What we decline, and why declining fast is a skill

Say no in the first 60 seconds and refer it out. A referral costs you nothing and builds
a reciprocal network; a call you take and can't finish costs you a night and a review.

- **Gas leaks, active electrical fire, structural collapse in progress** → 911 / utility
  first, always. Never dispatch into these.
- **Sewage (Category 3 water)** without proper certification and PPE → refer to a
  restoration specialist, then bid the rebuild.
- **Asbestos or lead abatement** → licensed abatement contractors only. Full stop.
- **Licensed-trade emergencies** (main panel, gas line, main sewer) → your subcontractor
  network. **Refer it, then bid the carpentry repair after.** This is a revenue channel,
  not a loss.

Build the referral list *before* launch, and ask each one to refer carpentry back.

### Emergency pricing structure

| Component | Standard hours<br>(Mon–Fri 7a–5p) | After hours<br>(nights, Sat) | Premium<br>(Sun, holidays) |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Dispatch fee** (includes first hour on site) | $175 | $275 | $375 |
| **Additional labor** — lead tech | $125/hr | $185/hr | $250/hr |
| **Additional labor** — helper | $75/hr | $110/hr | $150/hr |
| **Materials** | Cost + 30% | Cost + 30% | Cost + 35% |
| **Equipment** (dehumidifier, air mover, generator) | Daily rate | Daily rate | Daily rate |

**Rules that keep this profitable:**

- **The dispatch fee is charged the moment we roll**, whether or not the job proceeds.
  It is disclosed on the phone before dispatch, every time, no exceptions. This kills
  tire-kickers and pays for the truck.
- **Minimum 2-hour billing after hours.** Waking a tech at 2am for a 40-minute job is a
  net loss unless you bill for it.
- Labor is billed **portal to portal** after hours — drive time is real time.
- **All time is billed in 30-minute increments**, rounded up.
- Equipment placed on site (air movers, dehumidifiers) bills daily until picked up.
  Track it — unbilled equipment days are pure lost margin, and lost equipment is worse.

### Insurance claim work

A large share of emergency work is reimbursed by the homeowner's carrier. Handling this
well is a genuine competitive moat, because most small contractors handle it badly.

**How the money actually works.** The homeowner is your customer and owes you the money.
Their insurer reimburses *them*. Your job is to make that reimbursement flow to you
smoothly, which means: document like an adjuster will read it, price like an adjuster
expects, and get paperwork signed that lets the carrier pay you directly.

- **Document obsessively.** Wide shots, close shots, moisture meter readings, serial
  numbers on equipment, timestamps, before/during/after. A claim is won or lost on
  photos. Standard on every emergency call — no discretion. → [§4.4](04-operations-manual.md#44-documentation-standard)
- **Get the Work Authorization signed before touching anything.** This is your contract,
  and it authorizes emergency mitigation at your rates. → [forms/work-authorization.md](../forms/work-authorization.md)
- **Get a Direction to Pay signed** where your state permits it, so the carrier can pay
  you directly rather than routing through the homeowner.
- **Learn Xactimate** (~$150–$300/mo). It is the estimating standard nearly every carrier
  uses. Submitting a properly formatted Xactimate estimate is the difference between
  getting paid line-item and arguing for three months. **This is the single highest-ROI
  software purchase in the business.**
- **Get IICRC WRT certified** (Water Damage Restoration Technician, ~$600, 3 days). It is
  what adjusters look for before they trust your mitigation invoice.
- **Never advance the deductible or "waive" it.** It is insurance fraud in most states
  and will end the company.
- **Never sign an AOB** (Assignment of Benefits) without an attorney reviewing it. Several
  states restrict or prohibit them, and they shift the entire claim risk onto you.

**Build adjuster relationships deliberately.** Adjusters refer work to contractors who
make their files easy. Three good adjuster relationships are worth more than $30K of
advertising. Introduce yourself, submit clean documentation, respond same-day, never
inflate a scope. → [§7.4](07-marketing-and-sales.md#74-referral-and-partner-channels)

|---|---|---|

| Deck build — composite | 300–500 sq ft, hidden fasteners | $75 – $130 / sq ft |
| Deck repair / reboard | Existing frame, new surface & rail | $3,500 – $12,000 |
| Bathroom remodel | Full gut, standard footprint | $12,000 – $35,000 |
| Kitchen remodel | Full gut, semi-custom cabinets | $28,000 – $75,000 |
| Basement finishing | 600–1,000 sq ft | $35,000 – $80,000 |
| Framing & structural repair | Beam, joist, sill, header replacement | $2,500 – $18,000 |
| Siding & exterior trim | Partial to full elevation | $6,000 – $30,000 |
| Door & window replacement | Per opening, installed | $600 – $2,400 |
| Custom built-ins & millwork | Shelving, benches, mantels, trim | $1,800 – $9,000 |
| **Insurance restoration rebuild** | Post-mitigation reconstruction | $8,000 – $60,000 |

### Pricing method: fixed price, built bottom-up

Never bid remodeling hourly — you absorb every inefficiency and the customer has no cost
certainty. Build every fixed price the same way, every time:

```
   Direct labor hours × burdened labor rate     ← §5.3
 + Materials at cost                            ← actual quotes, not memory
 + Subcontractor quotes                         ← written, not verbal
 + Equipment & dumpster & permits
 ─────────────────────────────────────
 = DIRECT JOB COST
 + Overhead recovery      (18–22% of direct cost)
 + Profit                 (12–18% of direct cost)
 + Contingency            (5% standard / 10% on any pre-1980 home)
 ─────────────────────────────────────
 = CONTRACT PRICE
```

**Four rules that protect the margin:**

1. **Never bid from memory.** Get written material quotes and written sub quotes on every
   job over $5,000. Lumber prices move fast enough to erase a margin between bid and build.
2. **Contingency is not padding, and it is not optional.** Older homes hide rot, knob-and-tube,
   undersized joists, and no header where a header should be. 10% on anything pre-1980.
3. **Markup vs. margin — get this right.** To hit a 40% *margin* you multiply cost by
   1.667, not 1.40. Marking cost up 40% yields a 28.6% margin. This single arithmetic
   error has killed more contractors than bad weather.
4. **Price is not a negotiation over your profit.** If the customer needs a lower number,
   reduce the *scope*, not the margin. Cutting your profit to win work is how you end the
   year busy and broke.

### Payment terms — non-negotiable

| Contract size | Deposit | Draw schedule | Final |
|---|---|---|---|
| Under $2,500 | None | — | Due on completion |
| $2,500 – $10,000 | 30% at signing | 40% at material delivery / rough-in | 30% at completion |
| $10,000 – $40,000 | 25% at signing | 25% mobilization · 25% rough-in · 15% at finish start | 10% at punch-list sign-off |
| Over $40,000 | 20% at signing | Milestone draws, max 3 weeks apart | 10% retainage released at sign-off |

- **Check your state's deposit cap.** Several states limit residential deposits (a
  percentage of contract or a flat dollar ceiling). Exceeding it can be a license
  violation. → [§8.1](08-risk-insurance-compliance.md#81-licensing-the-fill-in-the-blank-checklist)
- **Never let the work get ahead of the money.** If a draw is late, work stops that day —
  politely, in writing, per the contract. The instinct to "just keep going and sort it
  out" is how contractors end up financing a stranger's remodel.
- **Change orders are signed and paid before the work happens.** Every time. → [forms/change-order.md](../forms/change-order.md)

## 3.3 The bridge: converting emergency calls into rebuild contracts

This is the highest-leverage process in the company and it deserves an explicit script,
not improvisation at midnight.

**The mistake to avoid:** hard-selling a remodel while a homeowner is standing in two
inches of water. It reads as vulture behavior, it damages trust, and it loses the job.

**The sequence that works:**

1. **On site, night one — solve the problem and say nothing about the rebuild.** Stop the
   damage. Be calm and competent. Competence at 2am *is* the sales pitch.
2. **Before leaving — plant one sentence.** *"We've got it stopped and secured. Once the
   adjuster's been out, we do the repair side too — I can walk you through what the
   rebuild looks like whenever you're ready. No rush tonight."* That's the entire pitch.
   Then leave.
3. **Next morning — deliver the documentation package.** Photos, description of what was
   done, the mitigation invoice, and a short plain-English note on what to expect from the
   claim process. This single email is worth more than any advertisement you will ever
   buy. It arrives when the homeowner is overwhelmed and it makes you the competent one.
4. **Day 3–5 — call about the adjuster, not about the job.** *"Did the adjuster get out?
   How'd it go?"* Offer to meet the adjuster on site. Homeowners find adjuster meetings
   intimidating; showing up is enormous goodwill and puts you in the room where the scope
   is decided.
5. **Day 5–10 — present the rebuild estimate.** By now you're not a vendor, you're the
   person who's been helping. Present a clean scope with a line-item price.
6. **If they don't move — a 30-day and 90-day follow-up.** Insurance rebuilds routinely
   stall for months. The contractor still on the list when it un-stalls wins.

**Track conversion by technician.** Some people naturally build this trust and some
don't. That's coachable, but only if you measure it. → [§4.7](04-operations-manual.md#47-the-weekly-scorecard)

## 3.4 Maintenance agreements (Year 2 initiative)

Not material in Year 1 — but plan for it, because recurring revenue is the difference
between a job and an asset.

**`[COMPANY] HomeGuard` — $39/month or $420/year:**
- Annual home inspection (deck, roof visual, caulking, seals, water heater, sump)
- Priority emergency dispatch — front of the queue
- Waived dispatch fee on the first emergency call each year
- 10% off all repair labor
- Direct line to a named contact

**Why it matters:** predictable monthly revenue, a warm list to sell remodels into, and
higher business valuation at exit. Sell it to every emergency and remodel customer at
job close. A 15% attach rate on 500 lifetime customers is $31K/year of recurring revenue
against near-zero incremental cost.

## 3.5 What we do *not* do

Being clear about this prevents unprofitable detours and protects the brand:

- Commercial and multi-family (different insurance, different licensing, different
  payment behavior, prevailing wage exposure) — **revisit in Year 3**
- New home construction from the ground up
- Roofing as a standalone trade (we tarp and dry-in; full roof replacement goes to a
  roofing partner who refers carpentry back)
- Licensed trades in-house: electrical, plumbing, HVAC — **subcontract these**
- Asbestos, lead, or mold *abatement* (we can do lead-safe RRP renovation with
  certification — that's different from abatement)
- Anything requiring a license we do not hold

## 3.6 How to calibrate these numbers to your market

Do this in week one. Publishing a rate card built on national averages is how you end up
either the most expensive company in town or the busiest one losing money on every job.

1. **Call three competitors as a homeowner.** Ask for their after-hours emergency rate
   and their dispatch fee. Write it down. This takes 20 minutes and is the single most
   useful market research you will do.
2. **Get a real lumber quote** from your local supplier for a standard 16' deck package.
   Material cost drives 35–45% of a deck price and varies enormously by region.
3. **Get your actual workers' comp rate** from your broker for carpentry class codes.
   It swings from roughly 6% to over 20% of payroll by state and directly sets your
   burdened labor rate. → [§5.3](05-staffing-and-crew.md#53-the-labor-burden-math)
4. **Check three local remodelers' published deck pricing.** Most publish a per-square-foot
   range on their site.
5. **Then rebuild the rate card**, and re-run [§6](06-financial-model.md) with real numbers.

> **Position at or slightly above the local median, never below it.** You are selling
> 90-minute response, full insurance coverage, and a licensed crew. Competing on price
> against uninsured handymen is a race you win by going out of business. When someone
> says you're expensive, the answer is *"we are — here's what that buys you,"* not a
> discount.
