# 2. Formation & Ownership

## 2.1 Naming

Pick a name that survives both divisions. A name built only around emergencies caps you
at emergencies; a name built only around carpentry undersells the 24/7 differentiator.

**Test any candidate against these five:**

1. Does it work on a truck, a yard sign, an invoice, and a voicemail greeting?
2. Can someone spell it after hearing it once on the phone at 11pm?
3. Is the `.com` available and is the name clear on your state's business entity search?
4. Does it avoid words your state restricts (many states restrict "Engineering,"
   "Construction Company" without a license, "Insurance," "Bank")?
5. Does it avoid boxing you in geographically if you expand to a second metro?

**Structural patterns that work well here:**

- `[Name] Restoration & Carpentry LLC` — signals both divisions, insurance-friendly
- `[Name] Home Services LLC` with DBAs `[Name] 24 Emergency` and `[Name] Custom Carpentry`
- `[Name] Builders LLC` — remodeling-forward, use a DBA for the emergency brand

**Recommendation:** one LLC, one legal name, and **two DBAs** — one emergency-facing,
one remodeling-facing. Homeowners in crisis and homeowners planning a kitchen respond to
different messaging, but you only want one entity, one insurance policy, one payroll, and
one set of books. DBA registration is cheap (typically $10–$100) and reversible.

Reserve the name, the `.com`, and the Google Business Profile the same day. Do not skip
the entity-name search on your Secretary of State site before printing anything.

## 2.2 Entity formation — the actual checklist

Do these in order. Steps 1–4 can genuinely be done in one evening online.

| # | Step | Where | Typical cost | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Confirm name availability | Secretary of State business search | Free | Also check USPTO TESS for conflicting marks |
| 2 | File Articles of Organization | Secretary of State (online) | $50 – $500 | Most states approve same-day or 1–3 days online |
| 3 | Appoint a registered agent | Self or service | $0 – $150/yr | Use a service if you don't want your home address public |
| 4 | Get the EIN | IRS.gov (free, instant) | $0 | **Never pay a third party for this.** Takes 10 minutes |
| 5 | **Draft the Operating Agreement** | Attorney | $800 – $2,500 | See §2.3 — do not use a free template for a 3-party deal |
| 6 | Open the business bank account | Any business bank | $0 | Requires Articles + EIN + Operating Agreement |
| 7 | Open a business credit card | Same bank | $0 | Materials float; also builds business credit separate from personal |
| 8 | Register for state tax accounts | State Dept. of Revenue | $0 – $50 | Sales/use tax, withholding, unemployment insurance |
| 9 | Contractor license / registration | State contractor board | Varies widely | **See [§8.1](08-risk-insurance-compliance.md#81-licensing-the-fill-in-the-blank-checklist)** |
| 10 | Local business license | City / county | $25 – $400 | Required in most municipalities |
| 11 | Bind insurance | Broker | See [§8.2](08-risk-insurance-compliance.md#82-the-insurance-stack) | **Nothing happens before this** |

**Tax election note.** Default LLC taxation is pass-through. Once the company is
consistently producing owner compensation above roughly $70–$80K per working owner, an
**S-corp election** (IRS Form 2553) typically saves meaningful self-employment tax by
splitting owner pay into reasonable salary plus distributions. It is not worth the payroll
complexity in month one. **Revisit at the end of Year 1 with a CPA** — and note the
election generally must be filed within 2.5 months of the start of the tax year you want
it to apply to, so calendar this rather than discovering it in April.

## 2.3 Ownership structure

Three parties with genuinely different contributions: one supplies capital, two supply
labor and management. Splitting equity evenly three ways is the most common mistake here,
because it ignores that capital is a one-time contribution while operating is a daily one.

**Recommended structure:**

| Member | Class | Equity | Role |
|---|---|---|---|
| Operator A (Branden) | Class A (voting) | 32.5% | Co-Manager — Operations & Production |
| Operator B (Partner) | Class A (voting) | 32.5% | Co-Manager — Sales, Estimating & Admin |
| Capital Partner (Investor) | Class B (limited voting) | 35% | Capital, strategic advisory, no daily control |

**With a preferred return layered on top of it:**

> Distributions flow in this order: (1) 100% to the Capital Partner until invested
> capital is fully returned, plus an 8% annual preferred return on unreturned capital;
> (2) thereafter, pro-rata by ownership percentage.

**Why this shape.** The investor's real economics come from getting money back *first*
and earning a return on it — not from controlling the company. The operators' economics
come from owning most of the upside they personally create. This structure gives each
party what they actually want and prevents the classic failure where a passive investor
holds 51% and starts directing jobs they've never visited.

**Operators must also take a market-rate salary** for their work, paid as an operating
expense *before* profit distributions. Equity is not compensation for showing up. If
Operator A works 55 hours a week, that labor is a cost of goods, not an investment. Build
it into the P&L (it is — see [§6.3](06-financial-model.md#63-operating-overhead)) or you
will systematically overstate profitability and understate your bid prices.

**Vesting.** Operator equity should vest over 3–4 years with a 1-year cliff. If one
operator walks at month five, the company should not be permanently owned 32.5% by
someone who is gone. This protects the *remaining* operator as much as the investor.

### Alternative: structure the investment as debt

If the investor would rather have certainty than upside, a promissory note at 9–12%
interest with a 12-month interest-only period, secured by company assets, works cleanly.
Operators then own 50/50. This is simpler, avoids a three-party operating agreement, and
avoids the deadlock problem entirely. **Ask the investor directly which they want** —
"return of capital with a preferred return" and "equity partner" are very different
deals, and it is far cheaper to learn the difference now than in year three.

## 2.4 Operating agreement — the clauses that actually matter

Do not use a free online template for a three-party deal with unequal contributions. Pay
an attorney $1,500. The clauses that matter most:

- **Capital contributions & the preferred return waterfall** — exact dollar amounts,
  exact distribution order, in writing.
- **Management authority** — that Class A members manage day-to-day; enumerate the
  specific "major decisions" requiring the investor's consent (selling the company,
  taking on debt above a stated threshold, admitting new members, changing the business
  line, distributions above a threshold). Keep that list *short*. Everything not on it
  is the operators' call.
- **Deadlock resolution** — with two equal operators, a 50/50 split on a Class A vote is
  a real possibility. Specify the tiebreak: mediation, then a buy-sell trigger.
- **Buy-sell / exit** — what happens on death, disability, divorce, bankruptcy, or a
  member simply wanting out. Specify the valuation method *now*, while everyone is
  friendly. A multiple of trailing-12-month EBITDA is the standard approach.
- **Distributions policy** — including mandatory tax distributions so no member owes tax
  on phantom income they never received in cash. This one bites pass-through entities
  constantly.
- **Non-compete / non-solicit** — a departing member should not be able to take the crew
  and the customer list across the street. Scope it narrowly enough to be enforceable in
  your state.
- **Roles & time commitment** — write down that operators work full time and the investor
  does not. It prevents a year-three argument about who did what.

## 2.5 The worker problem — read this first

**This is the highest-risk item in the entire plan, and it is unresolved today.**

The investor "has the workers." That sentence describes a relationship, not a legal
structure, and there are only three legitimate versions of it. You must pick one
explicitly, in writing, before the first job.

### Option A — They become W-2 employees of the new LLC ✅ Recommended

The LLC hires them directly. It runs payroll, withholds taxes, carries workers' comp
covering them, and directs their work.

- **Pros:** Cleanest. Full control over scheduling, quality, training, and dispatch —
  which an emergency response business absolutely requires. No misclassification exposure.
  Insurance is straightforward. Builds a real, sellable company asset.
- **Cons:** Highest cash cost. Payroll burden runs ~25–45% on top of base wages (see
  [§5.3](05-staffing-and-crew.md#53-the-labor-burden-math)). Requires payroll processing
  and unemployment insurance registration.
- **Verdict:** Correct for anyone on your on-call rotation or working under your direction
  daily. You cannot run a 90-minute emergency SLA with people you are not allowed to direct.

### Option B — They remain employees of the investor's existing entity, which subcontracts to us

The investor's company invoices the LLC for crew labor as a genuine subcontractor.

- **Requirements, all of them, every time:** a signed subcontractor agreement; a current
  Certificate of Insurance naming us as additional insured; proof of *their* workers'
  comp coverage; their own contractor license where required; and genuine independence —
  they control how the work gets done.
- **The trap:** if we set their hours, supply their tools, direct their methods, and
  they work only for us, they are our employees regardless of the paperwork. The IRS,
  your state labor department, and your workers' comp carrier will each reach that
  conclusion independently.
- **The audit that actually gets you:** at your annual workers' comp audit, the carrier
  will ask for a COI for every subcontractor you paid. **For every sub without valid
  comp coverage, the carrier charges you premium as if they were your employees** — often
  a five-figure surprise bill in a single letter.

### Option C — Genuinely independent trade subcontractors 🔧 Use for specialty trades

Licensed electricians, plumbers, HVAC, roofers, drywall crews — each running their own
business, bidding jobs, carrying their own insurance and licenses.

- **This is normal and correct** for licensed trades you don't employ.
- Same non-negotiable rules: signed agreement + current COI + license verification
  **before they set foot on a jobsite.** No exceptions, no "we'll get it Monday."

### What to do tonight

1. Ask the investor directly: *"Are these people going on our payroll, or are they staying
   on yours and subcontracting to us?"* There is no third answer, and the plan's cost
   structure changes materially depending on which it is.
2. **Default to Option A (W-2)** for core carpenters and anyone on the emergency on-call
   rotation. The control you need for a 90-minute response SLA is legally incompatible
   with independent-contractor status.
3. Use Option C for licensed specialty trades.
4. If the investor pushes for Option B for the core crew, get an employment attorney to
   review it before you file the LLC — and price the risk honestly.

> The financial model in [Section 6](06-financial-model.md) assumes **Option A** and
> carries the full W-2 burden. If you go a different route, the labor lines change and
> the risk section grows. That is a deliberate, conservative choice.

## 2.6 Records, books, and the boring stuff that protects the LLC

The liability shield is not automatic — it is earned by behaving like a separate company.
Courts pierce LLC veils for exactly these failures:

- **Never commingle funds.** No personal expenses on the business card, ever. If you need
  money, take a documented distribution or salary.
- **Capitalize adequately.** An LLC with no assets and no insurance running a construction
  crew is an invitation to personal liability.
- **Document decisions.** Annual member meeting minutes, written consents for major
  decisions. Fifteen minutes a year.
- **Sign everything in the company's name** — *"Branden [Last], Manager, [Company] LLC"* —
  never your name alone. Signing personally can create personal obligation.
- **File the annual report.** Every state requires one. Missing it administratively
  dissolves the LLC, and an administratively dissolved LLC provides no liability
  protection at all. Calendar it the day you form.
- **Books from day one.** QuickBooks or equivalent, set up with **job costing enabled**
  before the first invoice. Retrofitting job costing onto six months of sloppy books is
  miserable and you simply won't do it. See [§4.6](04-operations-manual.md#46-money-controls).
