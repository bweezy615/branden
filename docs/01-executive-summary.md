# 1. Executive Summary

## 1.1 The business

`[COMPANY NAME]` is a licensed and insured residential repair contractor operating two
integrated divisions from a single overhead base:

**Division A — Emergency Home Repair (24/7).** Rapid-response service for homeowners in
crisis: burst and frozen pipes, storm and wind damage, fallen trees, roof leaks, failed
decks and stairs, sagging floors, break-in and vandalism board-ups, vehicle-into-structure
impacts, water intrusion, and emergency securing of doors, windows, and openings. We
guarantee a live human answer 24/7/365 and a technician on site within 90 minutes inside
our service radius.

**Division B — Carpentry, Decks & Remodeling.** Full-scope wood-centered construction:
deck design and build, deck rebuild and restoration, kitchen and bathroom remodels,
basement finishing, framing and structural repair, siding and trim, custom built-ins,
door and window replacement, and full insurance-restoration rebuilds.

## 1.2 Why the two divisions belong in one company

This is the part the investor needs to understand, because it is the only thing that
makes this different from the 40 other contractors in the market.

**Emergency response is the cheapest customer acquisition channel in residential
construction, and almost nobody runs it deliberately.**

Consider what happens on a burst-pipe call. A homeowner has water in their ceiling at
11pm. They are not shopping. They are not collecting three bids. They will hire the first
competent, insured company that picks up the phone, and they will remember that company
for years. We arrive, stop the damage, document everything, and get paid for the
mitigation — frequently by their insurance carrier.

Then the real opportunity: that house now needs drywall, flooring, trim, cabinets, and
paint. The homeowner has an insurance claim funding most of it. They have a contractor
standing in their living room who already has their trust, already knows the damage, and
already has photos and measurements. **The rebuild contract is ours to lose.**

```
  Emergency call            Mitigation invoice         Rebuild contract
  ($0 acquisition cost)  →  $600 – $2,500          →   $8,000 – $60,000
       ~365/yr                 ~100% capture          ~25% of qualified calls
```

*"Qualified" means the ~35% of emergency calls involving damage that actually requires
reconstruction. A jammed door is a legitimate emergency ticket with no rebuild behind it.
Full funnel math in [§6.2](06-financial-model.md#62-revenue-build--year-1-base-case).*

A $340 marketing spend to win an emergency call that yields an $850 mitigation ticket is
break-even marketing. The same $340 producing a 25% shot at a $19,000 rebuild is a
**14x return**. That asymmetry is the business.

The reverse also holds: the remodeling division keeps crews billable during the quiet
weeks between weather events, which is what kills pure emergency-service companies.
The two divisions hedge each other's demand curves.

## 1.3 Market

Residential emergency repair demand is non-discretionary and largely recession-resistant —
a burst pipe gets fixed in a downturn. Demand is driven by housing stock age, weather
volatility, and insurance claim frequency, none of which correlate with consumer
confidence. Remodeling is discretionary and cyclical. Running both smooths the curve.

The competitive landscape splits into three groups, and there is a gap in the middle:

| Competitor type | Strength | Weakness we exploit |
|---|---|---|
| **National restoration franchises** (water/fire mitigation) | 24/7 capability, insurer relationships | Expensive, corporate, slow to schedule the rebuild, subcontract the carpentry to someone else |
| **Local remodelers / deck builders** | Good craftsmanship, competitive price | 8am–5pm, no emergency capability, booked 6 weeks out, will not answer the phone at 11pm |
| **Handyman services** | Cheap, fast | Often uninsured, unlicensed, cannot do structural or permitted work, cannot handle insurance claims |

**We are the only one that answers at 11pm *and* can frame, permit, and finish the
rebuild with our own crews.** That's the position.

## 1.5 The ask

**$250,000 in startup capital**, deployed as:

| Use | Amount |
|---|---|
| Working capital (payroll + materials float, 12 weeks) | $95,000 |
| Vehicles + upfit (2 response units) | $37,000 |
| Emergency response equipment & inventory | $26,000 |
| Tools & jobsite equipment | $22,000 |
| Launch marketing & branding | $28,000 |
| Technology, software, phones, website | $9,500 |
| Insurance deposits & bonds | $6,000 |
| Formation, legal, licensing, certifications | $3,500 |
| Contingency (10%) | $22,700 |
| **Total** | **$249,700** |

**Working capital is the single most important line and the one first-time contractors
underfund.** You pay crews weekly and buy materials up front; homeowners pay on draws
and insurance carriers pay in 30–60 days. Companies with full order books die in that
gap. Do not let this line get cut to buy a nicer truck.

## 1.6 Year 1 base case

| | Amount | % of revenue |
|---|---|---|
| Revenue | $780,000 | 100% |
| Cost of goods sold (labor, materials, subs) | $452,400 | 58% |
| **Gross profit** | **$327,600** | **42%** |
| Operating overhead | $302,600 | 39% |
| **Net operating income** | **$25,000** | **3.2%** |

Thin, and deliberately so — this is what an honest first year looks like when you are
building crews, buying trucks, and paying to make the phone ring. The return is in
Year 2, when revenue roughly doubles against overhead that grows maybe 35%, pushing net
into the 9–12% range. Full model, sensitivities, and break-even in
[Section 6](06-financial-model.md).

## 1.7 The three things most likely to kill this

Stated up front, because a plan that only lists strengths is a brochure.

1. **Worker classification.** The investor "has the workers." If those people are treated
   as 1099 subcontractors when they are functionally employees, the exposure is back
   payroll taxes, penalties, a workers' comp audit that bills us for every uninsured
   sub, and personal liability that can pierce the LLC. This must be resolved before
   the first job. → [§2.5](02-formation-and-ownership.md#25-the-worker-problem-read-this-first)

2. **Cash flow, not profitability.** Profitable contractors go bankrupt constantly. The
   defenses are non-negotiable: deposits before mobilization, draw schedules tied to
   milestones, change orders signed *before* work, weekly job costing, and lien rights
   preserved on every job. → [§4.6](04-operations-manual.md#46-money-controls) and
   [§6.5](06-financial-model.md#65-cash-flow-discipline)

3. **Answering the phone.** The entire acquisition thesis collapses if calls go to
   voicemail. This sounds trivial and it is the most common failure. Emergency callers
   hang up and dial the next number in under 90 seconds. → [§4.1](04-operations-manual.md#41-intake-the-most-important-90-seconds)

## 1.8 What "good" looks like at 12 months

- 365+ emergency calls answered, ≥98% live-answered
- ≥25% of qualified calls converted to rebuild or remodel contracts (~32 contracts)
- 42%+ blended gross margin, verified by job costing on every single job
- Two full crews running at ≥70% billable utilization
- 50+ Google reviews at 4.7★ or better
- Zero lost-time safety incidents
- Zero unpaid receivables over 60 days
- A named relationship with at least three insurance adjusters who call *us*
