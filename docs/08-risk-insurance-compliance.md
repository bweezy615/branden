# 8. Risk, Insurance & Compliance

> **This section is state-agnostic by design.** Every state regulates contractors
> differently. What follows is a checklist that applies everywhere plus exact instructions
> for filling in your state's specifics. **Complete §8.1 before the first job over $500.**

---

## 8.1 Licensing — the fill-in-the-blank checklist

### Where to get real answers (free, in one afternoon)

| Question | Where to ask |
|---|---|
| Do I need a state contractor license? | Search **"[your state] contractor licensing board"** — go to the `.gov` site only |
| What's the dollar threshold? | Same board. Many states require a license above **$500–$1,000** per job |
| Do I need a license bond? | Same board. Typically **$5,000–$25,000**, costs ~1–3% of face value annually |
| Local permits & business license? | **Your city/county building department.** Call them — they answer and it's free |
| Which work needs a permit? | Same building department. Ask specifically about decks, structural, and egress |
| Sales tax on materials or labor? | **[Your state] Department of Revenue.** Rules vary enormously |
| Preliminary notice deadline for lien rights? | **[Your state] mechanics lien statute.** Often **20 days** from first furnishing |
| Residential deposit cap? | Contractor board. Several states cap deposits by % or dollar amount |
| Right-of-rescission requirement? | Contractor board. Most states require a **3-day right to cancel** on in-home sales |

### Federal requirements (these apply in every state)

- **EPA Lead-Safe RRP Certification — REQUIRED.** Any renovation disturbing paint in a
  home built **before 1978** requires an EPA-certified renovator and a certified firm.
  Firm certification is ~$300 for 5 years; the 8-hour renovator course is ~$250. **Fines
  run to five figures per violation per day.** A large share of your remodeling and
  restoration work will be in pre-1978 housing. **Get this before you take a single
  remodeling job.**
- **OSHA compliance** — fall protection above 6 feet in construction, ladder safety,
  respirable silica standard, hazard communication.
- **I-9 verification** for every employee.
- **Federal EIN** and payroll tax deposits on schedule.

## 8.2 The insurance stack

**Nothing happens before this is bound.** One uninsured incident on one job ends the
company and reaches the members personally.

| Coverage | Limits | Est. annual | Priority |
|---|---|---:|---|
| **General Liability** | $1M per occurrence / $2M aggregate | $4,000 – $9,000 | **Mandatory — day 1** |
| **Workers' Compensation** | Statutory | 6–20% of payroll | **Mandatory — day 1** |
| **Commercial Auto** | $1M combined single limit | $3,500 – $7,000 | **Mandatory — day 1** |
| **Inland Marine (tools & equipment)** | $50,000 | $600 – $1,200 | **Day 1** |
| **Umbrella / Excess Liability** | $1M – $2M | $1,200 – $2,500 | **Day 1** |
| **Contractor's Pollution Liability** | $1M | $1,500 – $3,000 | Month 1 — water/mold work |
| **Installation Floater** | Varies | $400 – $900 | Month 1 |
| **Builder's Risk** | Per project | Per job | Per job, larger remodels |
| **Employment Practices (EPLI)** | $500,000 | $800 – $1,800 | Month 6+ |
| **Cyber Liability** | $500,000 | $600 – $1,200 | Month 6+ |

### Notes that matter more than the premium

- **Workers' comp is not optional and it is the one people try to skip.** Most states
  require it from the first employee. Going without it means personal liability for every
  injury, criminal exposure in some states, and license revocation. It is also the largest
  single variable in your cost structure — **get your actual class-code rate from a broker
  in week one** because it directly sets your bid prices
  ([§5.3](05-staffing-and-crew.md#53-the-labor-burden-math)).
- **Use a broker who specializes in contractors.** A general commercial agent will write
  you a policy with exclusions you won't discover until a claim is denied. Ask
  specifically about: water damage exclusions, **subcontractor coverage**, completed
  operations, and whether your emergency mitigation work is covered under GL or requires
  pollution liability.
- **Read the exclusions.** Many GL policies exclude mold, water damage from your work, and
  faulty workmanship. For a company doing water mitigation, those exclusions are the whole
  point of the policy.
- **Additional insured endorsements** — some customers and all commercial clients will
  require them. Know how to issue a certificate quickly.
- **Every sub carries their own.** No COI, no jobsite access. See
  [§5.6](05-staffing-and-crew.md#56-subcontractor-management) for why this is a five-figure
  issue at audit time.

## 8.3 Contracts — clauses that prevent disputes

Every project contract should contain all of these. Have an attorney draft the master
template once (~$1,000); it pays for itself on the first dispute avoided.

- **Scope of work** — specific, in plain language
- **Exclusions** — explicitly what is *not* included. The most valuable clause in the
  contract and the most commonly omitted.
- **Price and payment schedule** — deposit, draws tied to milestones, retainage
- **Change order clause** — no change without a signed written order at a stated price
- **Concealed conditions clause** — how discovered rot, mold, code violations, or
  structural deficiencies are handled and priced. **Essential in older homes.**
- **Schedule with force majeure** — weather, supply delays, permit delays
- **Right to cancel** — 3 days in most states, required, must be conspicuous
- **Warranty** — 1 year workmanship, materials per manufacturer
- **Lien rights notice** — required disclosure language in many states
- **Dispute resolution** — mediation first, then arbitration; specify venue
- **Termination** — what each party can do, and what gets paid on termination
- **Site access, work hours, cleanup standards, pet and child safety**
- **Photo release** — permission to use project photos in marketing (ask for it in the
  contract; nobody says no at signing, and it builds your portfolio for free)

**And for emergency work:** the Work Authorization is your contract. It must be signed
**before** work begins, must state the rate structure, and must authorize emergency
mitigation. → [forms/work-authorization.md](../forms/work-authorization.md)

## 8.4 Safety program

A serious injury is the most likely catastrophic event in this business. It is also the
most preventable.

**Program elements:**
- **Written safety manual** — issued and signed on day one of employment
- **OSHA 10-hour** for all field employees; **OSHA 30-hour** for crew leads and owners
- **Weekly toolbox talk** — 10 minutes, documented with signatures
- **Job hazard analysis** on every emergency call before entry — emergency scenes are
  genuinely dangerous: compromised structure, energized water, unknown conditions, darkness
- **PPE issued and required** — hard hats, eye protection, gloves, hearing protection,
  respirators where needed, high-vis
- **Fall protection above 6 feet.** Non-negotiable, no exceptions, no "it's just a quick one"
- **Ladder safety** — 4:1 ratio, three points of contact, tied off
- **Silica** — wet cutting or vacuum shroud on all concrete/masonry work
- **Lockout/tagout** before any work near electrical
- **Incident reporting** — every incident *and near-miss* reported same day, investigated,
  documented
- **Zero tolerance for impairment.** Immediate termination. Non-negotiable in an industry
  where it's common and where it kills people.

**Emergency-specific hazards** to brief on explicitly: electrical hazard in standing water,
structural collapse risk after impact, working at night with poor lighting, carbon monoxide
from generators (never indoors or in a garage), contaminated water categories, and working
alone. **Two-person minimum on any structural or water emergency after dark.**

**Track it:** zero lost-time incidents is a scorecard metric
([§4.7](04-operations-manual.md#47-the-weekly-scorecard)) and it's tied to the quarterly
safety bonus. Beyond the moral case, your workers' comp experience modifier directly sets
your premium for years — a clean safety record is worth tens of thousands of dollars.

## 8.5 Risk register

| Risk | Likelihood | Impact | Mitigation |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Worker misclassification** | **High** | **Severe** | Resolve [§2.5](02-formation-and-ownership.md#25-the-worker-problem--read-this-first) before launch. Default to W-2. |
| **Cash flow shortfall** | **High** | **Severe** | $95K working capital, $50K LOC, deposits, weekly AR review, stop-work on late draws |
| **Uninsured subcontractor at comp audit** | High | High | COI folder, expiration tracking, no COI = no site access |
| **Underbidding from bad estimating** | High | High | Job costing every job weekly; bottom-up estimates; 10% contingency on pre-1980 |
| Missed calls | High | High | 24/7 answering service, overflow routing, monthly audit of recordings |
| Key person dependency (2 owners) | High | High | Document processes, cross-train, key person life insurance, hire a coordinator early |
| Serious injury on site | Medium | Severe | Safety program, OSHA training, WC coverage, two-person rule after dark |
| Customer payment dispute | Medium | Medium | Written contracts, signed change orders, photo documentation, lien rights preserved |
| Working without a required permit | Medium | High | Permit check at Gate 5; when unsure, call the building department |
| Lead paint (RRP) violation | Medium | High | Firm + renovator certification before any pre-1978 remodel |
| Insurance claim denied / underpaid | Medium | Medium | Xactimate, IICRC cert, obsessive documentation, adjuster relationships |
| Crew turnover | Medium | Medium | Pay on time, good tools, real on-call comp, referral bonuses |
| Material cost spike between bid and build | Medium | Medium | Written quotes, price validity clause (30 days) in every estimate |
| Weather-driven revenue swings | Medium | Medium | Two divisions hedge each other; build backlog during storm season |
| Bad review / reputation hit | Medium | Medium | QC gates, 3-day callback policy, respond to every review in 24 hrs |
| Investor/operator conflict | Medium | High | Operating agreement: defined roles, short major-decisions list, deadlock clause, buy-sell |
| Economic downturn | Low–Med | Medium | Emergency work is non-discretionary; lean harder on it, cut marketing not crews |

**The top four are the ones that actually kill first-year contractors.** Everything below
them is manageable. Read those four rows again before you spend money on anything else.

## 8.6 Recordkeeping

Keep for the stated period, organized by job number:

| Record | Retain |
|---|---|
| Contracts, change orders, work authorizations | 7 years (or your statute of repose, whichever is longer) |
| Job photos and documentation | 7 years — this is your defense in any construction defect claim |
| Payroll records, I-9s, time records | 4–7 years |
| Subcontractor COIs and agreements | 7 years |
| Tax returns and supporting records | 7 years |
| Insurance policies | Permanently |
| Permits and inspection sign-offs | Permanently, by address |
| Safety training records, incident reports | 5 years (OSHA 300 logs: 5 years) |
| Warranty issuances | Warranty term + 3 years |

**Store digitally with automatic backup.** A shop fire that destroys your paper job files
is a second disaster on top of the first. CompanyCam or your CRM handles photos; use cloud
storage with versioning for everything else.
